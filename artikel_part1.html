#2. Watch: Limerick dairy farm slashes inputs with regenerative practices

Limerick dairy farmer Tom Stack made the decision to embark on his regenerative farming journey around 8 years ago.

After taking over the family farm at Ballyagran in 2012, Tom became increasingly disillusioned with conventional farming and felt he needed a change.

He converted to organic in 2018 and sought to transform his 146ac farm into a system resilient to environmental and financial shocks.

It was during that year's BioFarm conference when Tom learned about regenerative farming practices, including Korean Natural Farming (KNF).

"Back in 2017, I had a vet bill of €7,500, I had a feed bill of €26,000 and a fertiliser bill of €14,000. I just thought to myself, there has to be another way.

"Then I just looked at different principles of regenerative farming and KNF was the one for me," he said.

KNF focuses on reintroducing indigenous microorganisms (IMO) to create fertile soils that produce a high output without the use of synthetic fertiliser, herbicides or pesticides.

By using bacteria, fungi and other microbes gathered from his own land, Tom is drawing on allies already adapted to his farm's particular soils and climate.

The process begins with specially designed wooden boxes filled with rice being placed at various locations on the land to help collect the microorganisms.

Following several further steps, a liquid IMO (LIMO) rich in nutrients and enzymes is created which can then be sprayed on fields or crops to improve soil biology.

Tom is currently milking 65 cows and supplys his organic milk to Glenisk with significantly reduced external inputs.

Since transitioning to regenerative agriculture, Tom has seen a fall of around 96% in feed costs (with zero purchased feed for his cattle) and two-thirds in fertiliser, now only applying lime.

His cows are entirely grass-fed and he creates his own nutritional supplements for his animals using only natural inputs and processes.

The herd has been antibiotic-free since 2017 and returned zero bovine TB reactors at its most recent test. One of Tom's key priorities is building immunity in his animals.

Tom’s system typically runs between 93-114 head of cattle, with a stocking density of 1.3-1.5 livestock units (LU)/ha.

Given the structure of the farm, the yearly stocking fluctuates, but production remains stable at 200,000-220,000L per year or around 3,700L per cow.

From just one hectare of arable land, Tom produces up to 15t of a grain mix (peas, oats, barley and buckwheat) some of which goes to calf feed, with the rest used to create his on-farm organic sprays under KNF.

Carrying no purchased feed and no synthetic nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium has helped Tom to lower his cost base and allow the farm's economics remain steady.

Excluding subsidies, Tom's gross margin per litre ran at 40 to 58 cent across 2022 to 2025, above the national average of 30 to39 cent.

Output per hectare is lower by design meaning that the farm's gross margin per hectare is under the national average.

To address this, Tom has chosen to integrate the processing of some of his milk into his farm, rather than opting for higher productivity genetics in his herd.

"Everyone has their own journey and there’s so many ways. I think the first step is to be viable as farmers.

"It’s the knowledge and education. But everyone has their own farm, everyone has their own mind, they can do what they want.

"But I just think just make that step, make that step to be self sufficient in a way," Tom said.

Tom was among a group of Irish regenerative farmers who recently held farm walks on their lands in conjunction with the European Alliance for Regenerative Agriculture (EARA).

A report recently published by EARA entitled Regenerating Systemic Risk: A Case Study on Ireland argued that there are "shared systemic roots" connecting rising input costs, bovine TB, water pollution, and young farmer decline.

It said that a transition to "regenerating forms of agriculture" could address these issues at the same time.

The new report argued that this change is "achievable and economically viable".

#4. Prevention strategies for calf pneumonia as temperatures swing

With calves landing on the ground of winter milking farms and temperatures swinging, the risk of calf pneumonia will be on the rise.

During the week, temperatures were climbing as high as 16℃ by day and dropping below 4℃ in places by night; this paired with high humidity levels presents a period of high risk.

Pneumonia is one of the most common health problems affecting young calves. It damages the lungs and causes long-term performance issues and even death.

In fact, it is the most common cause of death in calves under 10 months-of-age, with 3% of all calves born dying from the infection.

Even for animals that survive the infection, it is a quite costly treatment, with the chances of the animal ever getting back to 100% health being slim.

Pneumonia leads to reduced live weight gains as well as reduced milk production in the animal's first lactation, a stage 14% of animals do not get to if they have had pneumonia at a young age.

Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment is crucial, along with quality prevention methods in place.

Pneumonia is considered a multifactorial disease, as it can be caused by viruses, bacteria, poor colostrum intake, poor hygiene, poor ventilation, overcrowding, bovine viral diarrhoea (BVD), or other factors.

If a calf contracts the infection, it can take two to three days to develop into pneumonia, with calves turning quite quickly from there on.

Farmers must keep a keen eye out for symptoms such as reduced feed intake, coughing, fever, dropping ears, or hollow sides.

If you do not notice these signs, pneumonia will become obvious via symptoms such as increased respiratory rates or water discharge from the calf's eyes and nose.

However, once you notice these signs, the infection has gotten to an advanced stage.

Pneumonia can be hard to treat, with antibiotics often rendered ineffective depending on the case.

However, as they say prevention is better than cure, with hygiene from the moment the calf is born being one of the best prevention methods.

Feeding adequate quality colostrum after birth is crucial, so follow the '1,2,3 rule'.

Ensure the calf continues to drink adequate volumes of that colostrum for the first four to five days after birth.

Ventilation plays a huge role in preventing pneumonia in calves, so ensuring the calf house has plenty of air, no draughts and a well-bedded dry lying area where effluent can drain away freely is crucial.

During the calving season, things can get hectic, but it is important to stay on top of the calf pens - do not let them get overcrowded and ensure you are grouping calves by age and size.

By maintaining an adequate stocking rate in the shed, you will reduce stress and improve issues such as feed intake.

Implement a vaccination strategy on the farm, ensuring calves are being vaccinated with a shot after two weeks-of-age, followed by a second after four weeks-of-age, and booster shots during risk periods.

#6. Tractor of the Year 2027: Who is competing for the top honours?

The Tractor of the Year (TOTY) awards are returning to Bologna, Italy for EIMA this year, offering a snapshot of where agri-machinery is heading.

TOTY awards highlights range from flagship tractors designed for high horsepower fieldwork to more specialised machines built around having an aptitude for certain tasks.

The awards rotate annually between Agritechnica in Hannover, Germany and EIMA - the International Agricultural and Gardening Machinery Exhibition - in Bologna, Italy (such as this year's 2027 TOTY awards).

But who is in the running to take home gold this year?

Previously just the Tractor Of The Year category with no horsepower restriction, the HighPower category is dedicated to tractors above 300hp, recognising the high-powered tractors in modern farming.

One contender for the award is Fendt's 1167 Vario MT Gen 2, a 680hp tracked behemoth that was unveiled last month at the Farm Progress show in Iowa.

CNH is represented by Steyr's Cervus CVT tractor range, which was previewed by the Austrian brand at Agritechnica 2025, becoming its flagship high-hp line.

Ranging from 360-435hp, a maximum travel speed of 60km/h and a new central tyre inflation system, a lot of care and attention has gone into this machine.

Now in its third year, the MidPower category is focused on tractors ranging from 150-280hp, and machines that balance power, versatility and efficiency.

The 2027 MidPower category is dominated by two global parent corporations.

One of these is AGCO, with Massey Ferguson's 7S.245 Mark 2 andValtra's T263, both of which use the 7.5L, six-cylinder, AGCO Power Core75 engine.

The other major corporation is CNH, with both the Case IH Puma 185 and the New Holland T7.270 Methane Power competing in the category.

The Puma combines the PowerDrive transmission with a 6.7L six-cylinder FPT 6 engine rated for 180-241hp.

Unveiled at Agritechnica 2025, sister-brand New Holland's T7.270 Methane Power tractor provides an option for farm businesses with an anaerobic digestion plant.

While anaerobic digestion is not currently widespread in Ireland, the sector is expanding, and this tractor shows potential for operations with access to methane as a fuel source.

TOTY's Utility category is for tractors with a maximum of four cylinders, maximum operating weight of 10,000kg and a power range of 70-150hp.

The Fendt 314 Vario Gen 5 will be looking to reclaim the Utility category award that it won back in 2020 with its last edition.

Meanwhile Landini will be hoping to win its fourth award in TOTY's history and first outside of the Specialised category with its 6-145 RS.

In addition to the 7S Mark 2 in the MidPower category, Massey Ferguson has the 5S.145 Dyna-VT challenging for the Utility award.

Announced last year at Agritechnica, customer deliveries of the 5S Dyna-VT began rolling out this summer.

Established in 1998 by Italian magazine Trattori, the TOTY award category winners are decided by a group of 26 independent journalists representing 25 European countries.

In a statement to Agriland, TOTY chairperson, Fabio Zammaretti said he believes the "international dimension is one of the greatest strengths of Tractor Of The Year.

He added: "Agricultural requirements can vary significantly across Europe and bringing together different experiences, perspectives and farming traditions allows us to evaluate tractors from a much broader point of view."

According to Zammaretti, there isn't one single feature that determines the winner, as the jury's objective is to identify the tractor "that offers the best overall combination of technology, performance, efficiency, innovation and practical benefits for farmers.

"Naturally, the evaluation criteria are adapted to the different categories. What makes a great HighPower tractor is not necessarily what makes a great Specialised or Utility tractor.

"Ultimately, we are not looking for the tractor with the longest list of features, but for the one that makes the most meaningful contributions to modern agriculture," he said.

#9. Co. Cork farm mediator’s liver transplant inspires Dublin Marathon effort

A young teacher will run today's (Sunday, October 25) Irish Life Dublin Marathon for the Irish Kidney Association (IKA) after her best friend, now a farm succession mediator, received the liver transplant that profoundly changed both their lives.

As a biology teacher at Educate Together in Dublin, Niamh teaches her students about organs and how they function, but Edel's experience gave the subject a deeply personal meaning.

Niamh said: “I teach my students about human anatomy, including organs and how important they are to the body, but Edel's story has given organ donation a very personal meaning for me.

"When you see what an organ transplant can mean to someone's life, it brings the science home in a very different way.”

Niamh chose to fundraise for IKA because of its work promoting organ donation and transplantation through the organ donor card, while also supporting people living with kidney disease and their families.

The friends are passionate about encouraging people to consider becoming organ donors and giving others the chance of a second chance at life.

The two women met during their first year studying agricultural science at University College Dublin (UCD) and became close friends almost immediately.

Edel had been diagnosed with an autoimmune disease at just nine years-of-age, which was managed with medication throughout her childhood and teenage years.

But at the beginning of her second year at UCD, Edel's health began to deteriorate and she eventually had to take time away from college and return home to Cork, while continuing to travel to Dublin for regular appointments at St. Vincent's Hospital.

A serious bout of sepsis in autumn 2016 left her critically ill and in an induced coma.

It was following this episode that she was placed on the transplant waiting list.

After recovering from sepsis, medics warned her about the symptoms to look out for and stressed the importance of going straight to hospital if they returned.

While Edel was waiting for a suitable donor, Niamh began her daily 11:11 ritual.

Every day, when the clock struck 11:11, she would make a wish that Edel would receive the call for her transplant.

By the time Edel received the call that a suitable donor liver was available, she had spent 22 months on the transplant waiting list.

Niamh explained: “I've always thought of 11:11 as a moment of synchronicity and a little reminder to stop, make a wish and focus on something positive.

"So, I started wishing at 11:11 every single day that Edel would get the call for her transplant.

"I couldn't do anything to change what was happening, so it became my little moment of hope for her."

She decided to make the significance of 11:11 permanent. Niamh had matching 11:11 bracelets made for the two friends and also had 11:11 tattooed on her side.

Just two hours before Edel got the call for her transplant, Niamh picked up the engraved bracelets from the jewellers.

Edel said: “When Niamh came to see me after my transplant with the bracelets, it was very emotional.

“I knew about the 11:11 and she used to send me the screenshots every day, but I didn't realise that her wish every single time was for me to get the call.

"Then the bracelets arrived a couple hours before I got it. It became something very special between us.”

After recovering from her transplant, Edel got a matching 11:11 tattoo on her side, making the symbol a permanent reminder of their friendship and the journey they had been through together.

Since her transplant, Edel who has become a passionate advocate for organ donation remains deeply grateful to the family of her deceased donor.

She now lives in Carrigaline, pursuing a career in farm succession mediation.

Until mid-October of this year she worked part-time as a patient support officer at the IKA Munster kidney support centre, close to Cork University Hospital (CUH).

For Edel, the work at the IKA’s Munster kidney support centre brought her journey full circle.

She said: “Being around people who have been through similar things has been really nice.

"It can be lonely because not everyone understands, and you don't always talk about it as the years pass.

"Being able to help other patients and families, particularly those with children, makes me think of my own parents and what they went through.

"I know how helpless they must have felt, so it's really special to be able to help others now," she added.

In 2019, Edel was a guest speaker at the national launch of Organ Donor Awareness week.

She will also be a guest speaker and sharing her story at the Gift of Life charity ball at the Radisson Blu Hotel, Cork on November 14.

Niamh started running around two years ago and quickly became hooked.

She completed the Dublin half marathon in September, her second half marathon, as part of her preparation for the full 26.2-mile challenge.

#11. Housing treatment starts with reviewing the grazing season

Housing is a useful point to review the parasite challenge cattle faced at grass.

Peter Howard, veterinary adviser at Boehringer Ingelheim explains that grazing history, performance records, testing and previous treatments can all help farmers and their vets decide what action is needed now, and what should change next year.

He said: “Housing is sometimes treated as a date in the dosing calendar.

"However, the best starting point is not the medicines cabinet, it’s a review of what happened during the grazing season."

Cattle may be brought into sheds carrying gutworm, lungworm and/or liver fluke infections without showing obvious signs.

At the same time, not every group of cattle will have faced the same challenge at grass.

The age of the animal, grazing history, weather, stocking pressure, and previous treatments given over the spring and summer can all affect the level of parasite risk.

It is important for farmers to remember that since December 1, 2025, all antiparasitic medicines for food producing animals in Ireland require a veterinary prescription.

“Having a clear record of what happened during the grazing season gives your vet better information on which to base that prescription.

"It can also help reduce unnecessary treatments for parasites that may not be present,” Howard said.

Review cattle group by group rather than assuming the same plan is right for the whole herd.

Which fields did each group graze, for how long, and what is the grazing history of those fields?

Were young cattle moved onto fields cut for hay or silage later in the season?

Were any new animals purchased, and could they have brought in a different parasite risk onto the farm?

Look back at when wormer treatments were given, which active ingredients were used, and whether cattle were weighed and dosed accurately.

Did you have any concerns about whether the product had worked after treatment?

Performance records add to the overall picture. Poor weight gain, loss of condition, scouring, coughing, or a drop in milk yield may all need investigation.

“These issues are not proof of a parasite burden on their own, but they can help the vet interpret test results and assess the risk,” Howard said.

“You can’t identify a sub-clinical worm burden just by looking at an animal.

"It’s why testing is such a useful tool, and can help you and your vet understand the risk your cattle have faced this season,” Howard said.

For calves and young cattle in their first season at grass, faecal egg counts (FECs) can show how adult gutworm egg output changed during the season, and what level of worm burden they have likely been carrying.

Regular samples every two weeks from turnout are more useful than one isolated result because the trend can show when pasture challenge was increasing.

Results can be considered alongside recorded liveweight gain, grazing history, and any worm treatments already given.

In adult dairy cows, immunity can keep gutworm egg counts low, so routine faecal egg counting is less informative.

A bulk tank milk ELISA test can instead indicate the milking herd’s level of exposure to the gutworm Ostertagia ostertagi by detecting antibodies.

It is important to remember that a bulk milk test is a herd or group level monitoring test.

It cannot identify individual cows that require treatment. Timing of the test also matters because antibodies can remain for several months after treatment.

Lungworm is not detected by an ordinary gutworm faecal egg count, so any coughing during late grazing or shortly after housing should be acted on quickly.

Lungworm needs prompt veterinary investigation and treatment if confirmed, to reduce the impact on lifetime performance.

“The aim is not to collect test results for their own sake,” Howard said.

“When you combine test results with what happened on the farm it can help you make better decisions on what to do now, and if any changes need to be made for the next grazing season.”

Look at whether cattle performed as expected, did treatments appear to work, and did the same fields or groups of animals repeatedly experience problems.

This may identify where closer monitoring, different grazing management, or a change in the treatment plan is needed next season.

Housing provides an opportunity to discuss the evidence with your vet and decide which parasites need to be targeted, which groups of animals need treating, and whether further testing or changes in management practices would be useful.

#15. AI is changing farming: Are farmers asking the right questions?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is now a feature on many farms, from smart cow collars to locomotion monitoring to laser weeders and automated slurry scrapers.

This may have made life easier for some farmers and producers, but before signing up to a new system, should farmers now be asking questions about privacy, data ownership, and who is responsible for what?

According to the president of the scientific-technical committee at the Italian Association of Artificial Intelligence Professionals (AIPIA), when it comes to AI, farmers rarely get a view from "someone with no stake in the sale”.

This is why Rafael Patron is urging farmers to ask the right questions before bringing AI onto their farms.

AIPIA, an independent non-profit based in Rome, brings together developers, data scientists, researchers and consultants, and keeps a public directory of AI professionals for organisations that need that kind of expertise.

Patron believes that farmers should look closely at what they think AI could deliver for them.

He uses the example of systems that use camera footage of housed cows to build an individual profile for each one.

But Parton said: “It does not see the cow at grass in July.

"It does not examine a foot, and it cannot tell you why a cow's mobility has dropped.”

In an example like this, Patron said farmers should ask the IT supplier which conditions the system is and is not designed to detect, and what happens to the score when cows are out for the summer.

He added: “Ask, too, about false alarms; a system that flags too many cows gets ignored within a month.

“One that flags too few gives a false sense of security, which is worse.”

According to Patron one issue that may not be exactly transparent is who owns the information gathered by AI systems.

He said: “Daily footage and scores for every cow add up, over a year, to a detailed record of how a farm is run: stocking, bedding, feeding routines, how quickly problems are picked up.

“If the system is part of a buyer's scheme, find out whether the buyer sees the raw scores, a summary, or only what the farm chooses to share.

“Ask where the footage is stored, how long it is kept, whether it is used to train the supplier's algorithm, and what you can take with you if you leave the scheme or change supplier.”

While Patron said that none of this is necessarily a reason to avoid a system, he believes it is important to get the answers to these questions in writing.

In the case of cattle monitoring AI, Patron said: “Before installation, agree with your vet which alerts will trigger a visual check and which will go straight onto the herd health plan, and decide who on the farm looks at the dashboard each day.”

He advises that AI connected to existing software can help, but only if the scores end up in front of a human with the time and authority to act on the information.

Patron said that farmers who get the most from these systems “will probably be the ones who already know their cows well and use the scores to settle arguments with data rather than to replace their own eyes”.

He also recommends that it could be useful to visit a farm that has run a similar system for a full year, "ideally one that looks like yours”.

#22. Opinion: The benefits of daylight saving for agriculture

The tradition of ‘daylight saving’ can be traced back to the early years of the 20th century, before the days of modern lighting systems.

Switching the clock back by an hour at this time of the year allows farmers to make better use of the light that is available during the early morning period.

Had this not been so, milk producers, for example, would be spending more than half their working day in total darkness during the autumn and winter periods.

Switching the clocks forward in the spring reflects the fact that daylight hours are on the increase again at that time of the year.

But tweaking with the clocks does not change the fundamental reality: daylight is on the wane at this time of the year.

As a result, farmers will be working for a considerable period of time in almost total darkness during the autumn and winter months.

Maintaining the highest levels of safety is critically important in this context.

So, it is imperative that all lighting systems, both inside and outside farm sheds, are totally fit for purpose as the nights close in.

Because of Ireland’s northerly latitude, the long days during the spring and summer months is one of our greatest farming assets.

It is a phenomenon that delivers high levels of crops growth, all of which is linked to the availability of high levels of light intensity during those critically important weeks in a crop’s production cycle.

This is why Irish tillage farmers traditionally produce high yields of grains relative to their counterparts in more southerly latitudes.

Now, obviously this was not the case in 2026: the drought that impacted on Ireland during the months of June, July and August saw to that.

All other things being equal, light intensity – and not ambient temperature - is the key contributor to overall crop yields.

Specifically, where tillage is concerned, there is growing evidence that cool conditions during the spring and summer months deliver that all important chance for cereal crops, including wheat, barley and oats, to reach maturity over a longer period of time.

In turn, this provides individual plants with the opportunity required to secure optimal levels of grain fill.

Under such circumstances, the end result will be higher yields and, invariably, better grain quality.

So, yes, there is very reason why we should cherish the dark days of winter, as they are nature’s harbinger of the glorious spring and summer seasons that always follow.

#40. From cattle to humans – what the experts are saying about Shamonda

People working in agriculture around animals need increased awareness regarding the Shamonda virus and be alert to infected livestock.

Dr. Connor Bamford from the School of Biological Sciences and The Institute for Global Food Security at Queen’s University Belfast was responding to the news this week that the Shamonda virus was detected in a person who died in France.

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) is carefully monitoring the situation as this is the first time ever this particular livestock virus has been detected in a human.

The only infection so far was in a patient receiving treatment for a serious illness that made them more susceptible to infection and complications than the general population.

The patient died earlier this month. Investigations into whether the virus caused or contributed to the patient’s death and how the infection was acquired are ongoing.

The virus is spread by midges and mainly causes mild illness in cattle, though it can cause abortions and birth defects in calves.

It was first identified in Europe in late July, and earlier this month it was detected on two dairy farms in Co. Cork. It is not a notifiable disease in Ireland or the EU.

Dr. Bamford from Queens University Belfast said: "The very recent and rapid spread of this virus across Europe, carried by biting midges, is increasingly concerning.

"A human infection in a patient who died is also a serious development, even if the person was immunocompromised.

"More work must be done to ascertain how exposed humans are to this infection, and any association with illness.

"Current understanding is that this is not of major concern to the majority of people in the general public given its association with biting midge of livestock."

However, he stressed that this merits increased awareness in individuals like agricultural workers working near affected animals.

At this point, authorities have said that there is no immediate concern for the general public.

However scientists admit that the report of a detection in a seriously ill person who died in France is both "surprising and unusual".

Dr. Gerald Barry, assistant professor of Virology and deputy director of the Univesrity College Dublin (UCD) One Health Centre said: "For some context it is associated with an immunocompromised person who was already ill.

"There haven't been any other human cases reported previously with this virus or genetically similar viruses to it. It typically causes disease in cattle and sheep, not humans."

"As a broader point, Europe is currently being bombarded with new viruses, particularly mosquito and/or midge borne viruses," Dr. Barry added.

"We are seeing more and more cases in humans and animals from different viruses and these cases are slowly moving northwards from the Mediterranean basin towards the UK and Ireland.

He explained that the risk to the general public appears very low for several reasons.

Shamonda virus has been circulating in animals, particularly cattle, but also sheep, goats, horses and alpacas, for many years, yet this is the first recognised human infection, suggesting that transmission to humans is extremely unusual.

“We also know that this patient was severely immunocompromised, which may have made them particularly vulnerable to an infection that would not normally cause serious illness in humans," Prof. Solomon added.
