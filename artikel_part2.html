#51. Rare Swed-Trac ‘tractor Ferrari’ with original parts up for auction

Swedish tractor brand Swed-Trac only built about 60 tractors before going bankrupt in 1987, but there is now one up for auction again.

The Swed-Trac tractor - one of only a handful still around - is currently available via Klaravik, an online Swedish auction marketplace for used machinery, tools, and vehicles.

Swed-Trac was founded by the Pihlgren brothers in the 1980s, in Arvika, Sweden, but did not make it out of that decade.

The tractors had an eye-catching black and gold livery, colours usually reserved for commemorative or anniversary models in the world of tractors.

The brand was aiming to offer a Swedish-built machine to fill the gap left by Volvo BM.

The specific Swed-Trac ST 1674 which is up for auction has had just one owner since 1994.

Mikael Larsson used the machine sparingly and, as a result, he said it has clocked up just over 2,000 hours.

Larsson called his Swed-Trac a "tractor unicorn" before delving into its history.

He said: "It really was a tractor ahead of its time. Big, high-horsepower tractors like this were unusual back then.

"With its Scania engine delivering close to 170hp, it was more substantial than most tractors when it came out.

"The previous owner had worked at Swed-Trac, so there's a close link to its origins.

"It has done very few hours and is in original condition, including the interior. It's well preserved.

"Given that there can only be a few left, it's undeniably rare," he said.

The components used in Swed-Trac tractors were the basis for the brand's identity and downfall.

Utilising reliable Scania and ZF parts, Swed-Trac aimed to build a durable tractor for Sweden.

Unfortunately, due to high component costs (which in turn led to high retail costs) and delivery delays, the brand struggled to get off the ground.

Larsson pointed out the Polish heritage of the tractor, as the cab and axles had both come from Warsaw-founded Ursus.

He said that the tractor "deserves" to become a museum piece.

"To me, this is a 'tractor Ferrari'. It's a true cult classic, and together with Volvo BM, a major piece of Swedish tractor history."

The auction of the tractor closes on the Klaravik platform on Wednesday, October 28.

#56. Tipperary farmers gather for training on tackling invasive species

More than 28 farmers gathered in Cappawhite, Co. Tipperary this week for a practical training event focused on giant hogweed control and water quality protection.

The event highlighted the important role farmers play in tackling invasive species and improving environmental sustainability on their farms.

Hosted by Tom and Geraldine Ryan, Moher East, Cappawhite, the national pilot event brought together farmers, agricultural advisors and environmental specialists to demonstrate how productive farming and environmental protection can work hand in hand.

The training was organised by Teagasc through the Agricultural Sustainability Support and Advisory Programme (ASSAP), the Farming for Water EIP and INVAS Biosecurity.

It was supported by the National Biodiversity Centre, Nore Vision and advisers from Arra-Tipp, Dawn Meats, ABP and the Kinisla advisory teams, and Tipperary County Council.

Invasive species expert Noel McCarthy of INVAS Biosecurity led the training, providing practical guidance on the identification, safe handling and control of giant hogweed, one of Ireland's most problematic invasive plant species.

Participants learned how to identify the plant at different stages of growth, implement effective biosecurity measures and safely carry out mechanical control where infestations are detected early.

“Giant hogweed poses a significant threat to biodiversity, land management and public safety,” McCarthy said.

“Early identification and intervention are essential to preventing its spread, and farmers have a crucial role to play in controlling infestations across the landscape.”

The event featured field demonstrations showing how mechanical control methods can be effectively applied during the early stages of plant development, while also highlighting the importance of preventing the accidental spread of seeds and plant material.

Attendees were also reminded of the serious health risks associated with Giant Hogweed.

The sap contains toxic compounds that can cause severe skin irritation and blistering when exposed to sunlight, potentially leading to painful burns, long-term scarring and increased sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) light.

The organisers of the event stressed the importance of using appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) and following safe work practices when handling or removing the plant.

Alongside invasive species management, participants viewed a range of water quality measures already in place on the Ryan farm.

Farmers heard how targeted actions can reduce nutrient and sediment losses to local waterways while maintaining productive and sustainable farming systems.

Roisin McManus of Teagasc outlined several measures supported through the Farming for Water European Innovation Partnership (EIP).

She explained how participating farmers can access practical solutions to improve water quality and biodiversity on their farms.

Measures discussed included riparian protection, targeted nutrient management, biodiversity enhancement initiatives and strategies to reduce runoff risks, including the closing off of watercourses to cattle, and the use of solar powered pumps and water troughs in the pasture.

Joan Martin of the Farming for Water EIP highlighted the importance of a coordinated catchment-wide approach to invasive species management.

“To effectively manage invasive species, we need to work from the headwaters downstream and ensure all stakeholders are involved,” she said.

“A catchment approach allows farmers, advisers, local communities, state agencies and environmental organisations to work together to prevent the spread of invasive plants and protect our rivers and biodiversity.

"Success depends on everyone playing their part across the entire catchment rather than addressing issues in isolation,” she said.

Contractors Oakwin carried out assessments and undertook a giant hogweed removal programme in the west Tipperary area under the Farming for Water EIP.

Last year, approximately 11.3km of the Cappawhite stream and 6km of the Toem stream were tended to.

Further infestations along the Toem, Cappawhite and Cahernahallia tributaries of the upper Mulkear/Dead River catchment in Cappawhite were identified and removed in 2026 in collaboration with stakeholders and property owners.

#60. Livestock disease Shamonda detected for the first time in a human who died

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) is carefully monitoring the situation after French authorities announced the first ever reported human infection with Shamonda virus (SHAV).

This is a disease that has until now only affected animals.

ECDC has stated that it is in contact with the French authorities and European partners to investigate the potential public health implications of this first human infection.

The only infection so far has been in a patient receiving treatment for a serious illness that made them more susceptible to infection and complications than the general population.

The patient died earlier this month. Investigations into whether the virus caused or contributed to the patient’s death and how the infection was acquired are ongoing.

The human infection follows the first ever detection of Shamonda in European farm animals in summer 2026.

In animals, clinical signs were reported from June, and the virus was identified in late July.

It has since been detected in farm animals in: Ireland; Austria; Belgium; France; Germany; Italy; Liechtenstein; Luxembourg; the Netherlands; Portugal; Spain; Switzerland and Jersey.

The virus in Ireland was detected in samples from three animals on two dairy farms in the south of the country

Suspicions of an animal health issue on those farms were raised when a drop in milk yield was noticed.

SHAV has previously been detected in animals in countries outside Europe.

SHAV typically spreads between animals through bites from infected midges, but the specific midge species involved in transmission in this case is still unknown.

In Europe, biting midges are generally most active from spring to autumn, with seasonal patterns varying by species, location and weather.

Given the cooling temperatures, the activity of most biting midge species is declining in Europe.

ECDC said it is following developments in close contact with the European Commission and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), which covers animal health and food safety across the EU.

The Centre is working with the relevant EU Reference Laboratory to strengthen testing capacity and offer additional support to member states to identify any other potential cases.

#70. Is your milk being collected often enough?

Milk production begins to significantly drop at this time of the year, meaning it takes longer to fill up the bulk tank.

With that, many farms may see milk collections go from daily or even twice daily, to once every two days.

Meanwhile, other farms will only have their milk collected every third day. But how often should your milk be collected?

According to the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine (DAFM), there is no specified frequency in which co-ops must collect milk from a contracted supplier.

The majority of co-ops will collect within a three-day window to ensure they are processing a high quality product.

However, some farms have reported that collections can be less frequent than every three days in the shoulders of the year.

Studies have suggested that milk can be stored in a bulk tank for up to four days when in the right conditions.

However, minimal deterioration of quality relies on uncontaminated milk entering the tank.

Realistically, even with all the proper precautions in place, the total bacteria count (TBC) in the bulk tank will rise at this time of the year, as unfavourable environmental conditions see the likes of somatic cell counts (SCC) rising.

There is a number of rules under EU legislation in place for farmers to ensure milk is stored safely ahead of collection.

Under Regulation (EC) No 853/2004, farmers are first obliged to carry out milking hygienically.

Then immediately after milking, the milk must be held in a clean place designed and equipped to avoid contamination.

For daily collections, it must be cooled immediately to 8oC or lower, or a maximum of 6oC if collection is not daily.

Milk leaves the udder at approximately 35°C; the ideal practice to maintain quality is reducing that temperature to 4-5°C within 30 minutes after milking finishes.

Having milk pass through a plate cooler can half the temperature instantly, bringing it down to approximately 18°C before entering the bulk tank.

When it is collected, the co-op is then obliged to ensure milk arrives at the processing plant at a temperature no higher than 10oC.

EU legislation indicates that TBC in milk must be less than 100,000/ml, with the majority of Irish co-ops penalising suppliers if their TBC is above 50,000/ml.

Realistically, TBC should be sitting in or around the 1,000/ml mark as it leaves the udder, and rising to approximately 5,000/ml by the time it reaches the bulk tank.

According to Teagasc, if this milk sits in a well-cleaned tank for two days at 4°C, that level is expected to rise to approximately 10,000/ml.

As collections push out to three days, the TBC is expected to rise to 30,000/ml.

However, during diet transitions and tricky environmental conditions, the TBC on many farms will be higher than 5,000/ml by the time it reaches the bulk tank.

That means if collection are only every three or four days, TBC may be bordering penalisation figures, and posing the question, how often should your milk actually be collected?

A total of 48 cases of bluetongue have now been detected in Ireland, the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine confirmed today (Wednesday, October 21).

New cases have been identified in Co. Cork, Co. Wexford, Co. Waterford and Co. Wicklow.

Latest figures show that Co. Wexford is currently the worst impacted county with 16 bovine cases and one sheep case.

Bluetongue is a non-contagious viral disease spread by biting midges.

According to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) rising temperatures have resulted in the growth of the disease across Europe.

The bluetongue virus is particularly difficult to control, according to WOAH, because it has more than 27 serotypes.

The serotype that has been detected in Ireland is 3.

Bluetongue is a notifiable disease and any suspect case must be reported to DAFM.

The department has urged farmers, veterinary practitioners and other relevant stakeholders to "remain vigilant for bluetongue".

According to DAFM sheep are more likely to show obvious clinical signs of bluetongue than cattle if they become infected.

It has also warned that mortality rates can be as high as 70%.

The Minister for Agriculture, Food and the Marine, Martin Heydon, believes that "vaccination is the best tool" that the country has to offset the losses which can be caused by the virus .

A total of four million doses of BTV-3 (bluetongue) vaccines have now been supplied to private veterinary practitioners in Ireland according to latest official figures.

The number of confirmed bluetongue cases have also risen in the UK this week.

There have been 3,426 cases of bluetongue serotype 3 (BTV-3) or unspecified serotype in the UK in the 2026 to 2027 bluetongue season.
